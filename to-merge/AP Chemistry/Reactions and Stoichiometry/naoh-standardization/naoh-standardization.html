NaOH Standardization Lab

Prepare a sodium hydroxide solution, then standardize it against a known mass of KHP.

Why standardize a base solution?

Solid NaOH is hygroscopic — it absorbs water from the air, so the mass you weigh out is never quite what the label on the calculation says it should be. That means the "nominal" concentration you calculate from mass ÷ volume is never fully trustworthy.

To find the true concentration, chemists titrate the NaOH solution against a carefully weighed sample of KHP (potassium hydrogen phthalate) — a stable, high-purity primary standard that reacts with NaOH in a clean 1:1 ratio.

In this lab you will:

  1. Weigh solid NaOH and prepare a solution in a volumetric flask
  2. Weigh a KHP sample and titrate it with your NaOH solution to standardize it
  3. Compare your nominal (mass-based) and standardized (titration-based) concentrations

Results: Nominal vs. Standardized Concentration

Notice that your nominal concentration (calculated from the mass of NaOH you weighed and the volume of the flask) is not the same as your standardized concentration (calculated from titrating against KHP).

That's because solid NaOH is hygroscopic and can also contain small amounts of absorbed carbonate — the mass on the label of your calculation is never perfectly reliable. KHP, on the other hand, is a stable, high-purity solid that can be weighed accurately and reacts with NaOH in a clean, known 1:1 ratio. Titrating against it tells you the true concentration of your NaOH solution.

This is exactly why analytical chemists standardize titrants before using them to analyze an unknown — skipping this step would carry the NaOH's weighing error into every result that solution is later used for.
