What am I looking at?

Add the 1s electrons on the left to start.

Each dot is one possible spot for a single electron. It is not a path — all we can do is describe where an electron is likely to be found around the nucleus of the atom.

Where an electron can be found depends on its energy. Higher-energy electrons are found farther from the nucleus, in higher shells.

s subshell: 1 orbital, a sphere.
p subshell: 3 orbitals (px, py, pz), a dumbbell along each axis.
Every orbital holds 2 e⁻.

How were these dots decided?

Nobody drew these shapes. They come out of one equation.

Start with the Schrödinger equation

It is the rule that describes an electron as a wave near a nucleus:

Ĥψ = Eψ

Plug in the setup, solve for ψ

We plug in one electron being pulled toward a positive nucleus, and solve for ψ (“psi”), the electron’s wave. The equation only allows a few clean answers, and each answer is one orbital — 1s, 2s, 2p, and so on.

That gives us a probability map

On its own ψ is just math. But ψ² at a point = the chance of finding the electron there. Large ψ² means the electron is often there; ψ² = 0 means never.

Build the 3D shape from the map

The program scatters points using that map: many where ψ² is high, none where it is zero. After thousands of points they pile up into the shape you see — a ball for an s orbital, a dumbbell for each p orbital. The shape is not drawn; it is just ψ² made visible.

(The p lobes here are drawn a little tighter than real life so the three are easy to tell apart.)
