NaOH and KMnO4 Standardization Virtual Lab

Two titration experiments: standardize a base against KHP, then standardize diluted permanganate against iron.

Why standardize a solution?

The concentration you calculate from the mass or volume you measured out (the nominal concentration) is only as trustworthy as the reagent. Solid NaOH absorbs water from the air; stock KMnO4 slowly decomposes and reacts with traces of organic matter. To get the true concentration you titrate against a carefully weighed primary standard — a stable, high-purity solid.

Experiment 1 — NaOH / KHP (1:1)

  1. Weigh solid NaOH and prepare a solution in a volumetric flask
  2. Weigh a KHP sample (primary standard) and titrate it with your NaOH
  3. Compare your nominal (mass-based) and standardized (titration-based) concentrations

Experiment 2 — Iron / Permanganate (1:5)

  1. Measure a calculated volume of stock KMnO4 and dilute it in a volumetric flask
  2. Weigh a sample of ferrous ammonium sulfate (primary standard for Fe2+) and titrate it with your diluted KMnO4
  3. Permanganate is its own indicator — the endpoint is the first permanent pink. The reaction is not 1:1: MnO4− + 5 Fe2+ + 8 H+ → Mn2+ + 5 Fe3+ + 4 H2O

Experiment 2 — Iron / Permanganate (1:5)

In both experiments your nominal concentration (from the mass or volume you measured out) is not the same as your standardized concentration (from titrating against a weighed primary standard).

NaOH is hygroscopic and picks up water and carbonate from the air, so the mass you weigh never matches the label. Stock KMnO4 is not a primary standard either — it slowly decomposes and is reduced by traces of organic matter, so the bottle’s stated concentration drifts over time. KHP and ferrous ammonium sulfate, by contrast, are stable, high-purity solids that can be weighed accurately.

Experiment 2 also shows that titration stoichiometry is not always 1:1: each MnO4− oxidizes five Fe2+, so the mole ratio has to be built into the calculation. Permanganate needs no added indicator — it is deep purple, and the endpoint is simply the first drop that leaves a permanent pale pink.

This is why analytical chemists standardize a titrant before using it — skipping the step carries the reagent’s error into every later result.
