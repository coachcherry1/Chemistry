An atom vs. a molecule

An atom is a single particle of one element. When atoms bond together they make a molecule.

H1 atom of H
H1 atom of H
O1 atom of O
H O H 1 molecule of water
This whole cluster = 1 molecule (H₂O). It has 3 atoms stuck together.

Key idea: a molecule can hold several atoms, but it still counts as one molecule.

Subscripts count the atoms

The little number after an element — the subscript — tells you how many atoms of that element are in the formula.

H2O
H H two H atoms
The 2 means 2 hydrogen atoms. No number on the O means 1 oxygen atom.

So H₂O contains 2 + 1 = 3 atoms in every molecule.

Atomic mass lives on the periodic table

Each element's atomic mass is the number listed under its symbol — the mass of one mole of those atoms, in g/mol.

8O16.00
Atomic mass of oxygen: 16.00 g/mol.

For these problems we'll round every molar mass to 2 places past the decimal to keep the arithmetic simple. Examples: H = 1.01, C = 12.01, N = 14.01, O = 16.00, Na = 22.99, Cl = 35.45.

Molar mass = add up every atom

To find the molar mass of a compound: multiply each element's atomic mass by how many atoms there are, then add.

CO2 C O O
C1 × 12.01= 12.01
O2 × 16.00= 32.00
Molar mass =44.01 g/mol
Add straight down the = column to get 44.01 g/mol.

What that number actually means

44.01 g/mol means: one mole of CO₂ molecules — that's 6.022 × 10²³ molecules — weighs 44.01 grams.

Molar mass is counted per mole of molecules, not per atom. One mole of CO₂ happens to contain 3 moles of atoms, but the molar mass still describes the whole molecule.

One more example — a bigger molecule

Glucose is C6H12O6. There are more atoms to add up, but the three steps don't change.

C6H12O6
C6 × 12.01= 72.06
H12 × 1.01= 12.12
O6 × 16.00= 96.00
Molar mass =180.18 g/mol
Count → multiply → add. 180.18 g/mol.

Your turn — the 3 steps

1. Count the atoms of each element using the subscripts.
2. Look up each atomic mass (2 decimals) and multiply by how many atoms.
3. Add the subtotals. That sum is the molar mass, in g/mol.

You'll do this for 5 compounds, typing the atomic masses yourself.
