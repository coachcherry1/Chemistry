# Combustion Analysis: answer key

Generated by `node tools/answer-key.js` from the model in `src/js/`. Do not edit by hand. SCHEMA 1.

Every student gets their own compounds and numbers (a seed saved with their progress), so this key gives the steps, the
rules, every compound and a worked sample set. Every number is computed by the activity from the real formula and checked by
`tools/validate.js`.

## The four steps (the teacher’s words)

1. **Percents to grams:** given percents, assume a 100 g sample, so 40.0% is 40.0 g. Given grams (or masses to subtract), use the grams.
2. **Grams to moles,** set up by dimensional analysis: g X × (1 mol X / ___ g X) = ___ mol X, the molar mass typed in the bottom of the
   fraction to the tenths place, so g cancels; then × (___ mol C / 1 mol CO₂), the mole ratio from the formula typed on top.
   Moles to at least 3 significant figures.
3. **Divide by small:** tap the smallest mole value, divide every row by it, to two decimal places.
4. **Multiply if not whole:** within 0.1 of a whole number, round (× 1). Otherwise the decimal is a fraction and every row is multiplied
   by the same number: .50 × 2; .33 and .67 × 3; .25 and .75 × 4; .20, .40, .60 and .80 × 5 (halves, thirds, fourths and fifths only).
5. **Molecular formula** (when a molar mass is given): empirical formula mass to the tenths place, n = molar mass ÷ it, every subscript × n.

In combustion analysis the rows are C (from the CO₂) and H (from the H₂O). Step 1 has no percents: the grams of CO₂ and H₂O are
used as they are. Step 2 is one dimensional analysis setup per row: g CO₂ × (1 mol CO₂ / 44.0 g CO₂) × (1 mol C / 1 mol CO₂) = mol C and
g H₂O × (1 mol H₂O / 18.0 g H₂O) × (2 mol H / 1 mol H₂O) = mol H; the molar mass and the mole ratio are the blanks.
The solution also checks g C + g H against the mass burned.

## Grading

- **The first Check of every step counts** (the teacher’s choice). The score is the steps right on the first check out of all the steps
  of the graded problems, sent as `cmi.core.score.raw` (0 to 100) after every step, with one `cmi.interactions` record per step
  (id `P3_C6H8O6_2moles`: problem, step number and name; what was typed; what was expected; right or wrong).
- **Not an answer** (the student is told and checks again, nothing is marked): an empty box; the right number rounded too far
  (moles to fewer than 3 significant figures, a quotient to fewer than 2 decimal places, grams with fewer decimals than the data,
  a molar mass not to the tenths place, 3.99 for a whole number).
- **Hints** are free. **Show me** appears after two misses in a step and fills it in (the step is already counted wrong).
- **Tolerance:** molar masses and moles within 1.5% (students use different periodic tables, as in mole conversions);
  quotients within 0.012 or 1%. Every box is judged against the student’s own earlier boxes, so one slip is counted once.
- `completed` on the results page, no `masteryscore`, retakes give new problems and the gradebook keeps the best score.

Graded set: 6 problems, comb, comb, comb, comb with a molar mass, comb with a molar mass, comb with a molar mass, in a mixed order opening with a problem that needs × 1 or × 2. Every set has a problem that needs × 1, × 2, × 3 and × 4 or × 5, and at least two molecular formulas with n above 1. No compound repeats or appears in a warm-up.

## The feedback each slip gets

| Slip | Feedback |
| --- | --- |
| A percent as a decimal (0.699 for 69.9%) | Assume a 100 g sample: 69.9% of 100 g is 69.9 g. Percents become grams with the same number. |
| The water’s percent as the salt (hydrate) | That is the water. Of the 100 g, the rest is the anhydrous salt: 100 − 36.1. |
| O₂’s mass for O | That is O₂. The oxygen here is O atoms in a compound, so use the atomic mass of O from the periodic table. |
| A molar mass not to tenths (159.61) | Round the molar mass of CuSO₄ to the tenths place, one digit after the decimal point (a whole number like 32 is fine when the tenths digit is 0). |
| Grams times molar mass (the bottom of the setup multiplied) | The molar mass is on the bottom of the fraction, so divide by it. Multiply across the top, divide by the bottom. |
| Molar mass over grams (the setup upside down) | Upside down: the grams go on top and the molar mass on the bottom, so g cancels and mol is left. |
| Moles to 2 significant figures | Keep at least 3 significant figures in moles (1.25): rounding too early can change the ratio. |
| 1 mol H per mol H₂O in the mole ratio | Look at the formula: H₂O has 2 H atoms, so every mol of H₂O holds 2 mol of H. |
| The mole ratio left out (mol H₂O as mol H) | That is mol H₂O. The mole ratio turns it into mol H: multiply by the 2 on top (2 mol H / 1 mol H₂O). |
| Dividing by the largest | Divide by small: every mole value is divided by the smallest one, so the smallest row is 1.00 and every other row is more than 1. |
| A quotient to one decimal place (1.3) | Keep two decimal places: one decimal place can hide the fraction (1.3 could be 1⅓ or 1¼, and 1 could be 1⅓). |
| × 1 when 1.50 is not whole | 1.50 is not within 0.1 of a whole number, so it cannot be rounded. Multiply if not whole: what fraction is .50? |
| × 2 for 1.33 | 1.33 × 2 = 2.66, still not whole. Read .33 as a fraction: its bottom number is the one to multiply by. |
| × 4 for 1.50 (all whole, not the smallest) | Those all come out whole, but they could all be divided by 2. Use the smallest number that works. |
| Rounding 1.50 to 2 instead of multiplying | Do not round 1.50: it is a fraction. Multiply it by 2 like every other row. |
| n upside down | Upside down: n = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass, so n is 1 or more. |
| Adding n to a subscript | Multiply every subscript by n, do not add n. |

## The worked example (step `four`)

A 1.129 g sample of an unknown hydrocarbon (only C and H) burns completely in oxygen. It makes 3.380 g of CO₂ and 1.845 g of H₂O. Find its empirical formula.

**Answer: C₃H₈.**

*Step 1: Percents to grams.* No percents: the grams of CO₂ and H₂O are given.

*Step 2: Grams to moles.* Molar mass of CO₂: 12.0 + 2(16.0) = 44.0 g/mol. Molar mass of H₂O: 2(1.0) + 16.0 = 18.0 g/mol. Every C atom ends up in a CO₂ (1 C each) and every H atom in an H₂O (2 H each). Check: 0.922 g C + 0.205 g H = 1.13 g, the mass burned (1.129 g): the sample was only C and H.

$$\text{C: }3.380\ \text{g CO}_{2} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol CO}_{2}}{44.0\ \text{g CO}_{2}} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol C}}{1\ \text{mol CO}_{2}} = 0.0768\ \text{mol C}$$

$$\text{H: }1.845\ \text{g H}_{2}\text{O} \times \frac{1\ \text{mol H}_{2}\text{O}}{18.0\ \text{g H}_{2}\text{O}} \times \frac{2\ \text{mol H}}{1\ \text{mol H}_{2}\text{O}} = 0.205\ \text{mol H}$$

*Step 3: Divide by small.* The smallest is 0.0768 mol (C): divide every row by it.

$$\text{C: }\frac{0.0768}{0.0768} = 1.00\quad \text{H: }\frac{0.205}{0.0768} = 2.67$$

*Step 4: Multiply if not whole.* 2.67 is 2⅔ (8/3), so multiply every row by 3.

$$\text{C: }1.00 \times 3 = 3.00 \to 3\quad \text{H: }2.67 \times 3 = 8.01 \to 8$$

$$\text{Empirical formula: }\text{C}_{3}\text{H}_{8}$$
