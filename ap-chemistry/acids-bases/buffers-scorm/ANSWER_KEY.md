### Checkpoint 1: What a buffer is

4 questions are drawn from a pool of 8.

- ★ `a-def` What does a buffer do?
  - **a. It keeps the pH from changing much when a little strong acid or base is added**
  - b. It keeps the pH at exactly 7, no matter how much strong acid or strong base is added to it
  - c. It stops strong acids and strong bases from reacting with anything else that is in the solution
  - d. It removes all of the H⁺ and OH⁻ from the solution so that the solution is always neutral
  - Why: A buffer does not stop the pH from changing and it does not hold it at 7. It makes the change small. The next parts show how.
- ★ `a-pair` Which mixture could be a buffer?
  - **a. CH₃COOH with CH₃COONa**
  - b. HCl with its salt NaCl
  - c. NaOH with its salt NaCl
  - d. HNO₃ with its salt KNO₃
  - Why: A buffer needs a weak acid and its conjugate base (CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻, which comes from the salt CH₃COONa). HCl, NaOH and HNO₃ are strong, and their ions Cl⁻, Na⁺, K⁺ and NO₃⁻ do not react with H⁺ or OH⁻ in water.
- `a-water` The same 4 particles of HCl are added to a flask of pure water and to a buffer. Which statement is correct?
  - **a. The pH of the pure water changes far more than the pH of the buffer**
  - b. The pH of the buffer changes far more, because it already has acid in it
  - c. Both pH values change by the same amount, because the same HCl was added
  - d. Neither pH changes, because HCl is a strong acid
  - Why: Pure water has nothing to react with the H⁺, so its pH drops from 7.00 to 1.40. The buffer goes from 4.74 to 4.38. The same HCl, but the buffer uses it up.
- `a-hcl-nacl` Why is a mixture of HCl and NaCl not a buffer?
  - **a. HCl is a strong acid, so Cl⁻ is not a base and there is no conjugate pair**
  - b. HCl is too weak an acid to react with any added base, so the mixture cannot absorb it
  - c. NaCl is a base, so it neutralizes the HCl and leaves nothing to absorb added acid
  - d. There is not enough water in the mixture to dissolve both of the compounds
  - Why: A buffer needs a WEAK acid and its conjugate base. HCl ionizes completely, so the flask holds H⁺ and Cl⁻, and Cl⁻ is far too weak a base to take H⁺ back.
- `a-nh` A solution contains NH₃ and NH₄Cl. Which statement is correct?
  - **a. It is a buffer: NH₃ is a weak base and NH₄⁺ is its conjugate acid**
  - b. It is not a buffer, because only a weak acid with its conjugate base can make one
  - c. It is not a buffer, because the base NH₃ and the acid NH₄⁺ cancel each other
  - d. It is a buffer only if there is more NH₄Cl than NH₃
  - Why: A buffer is a weak acid with its conjugate base, or a weak base with its conjugate acid. NH₃ and NH₄⁺ are a conjugate pair, so the mixture is a buffer.
- `a-both` Why does a buffer need both members of the pair?
  - **a. One member reacts with added acid and the other reacts with added base**
  - b. Together the two members make the solution exactly neutral
  - c. The two members react with each other to make water, which keeps the pH constant
  - d. Each member needs the other in order to dissolve
  - Why: The base member (Ac⁻) takes the added H⁺. The acid member (HAc) takes the added OH⁻. With only one of them, the buffer can resist a change in one direction at most.
- `a-acid-only` The diagram shows acetic acid in water with no acetate added. Is this a buffer? *(diagram: 10 HAc, 0 Ac⁻, nothing added)*
  - **a. No: there is almost no Ac⁻, so nothing is there to react with added acid**
  - b. Yes: HAc is a weak acid, and weak acids are buffers
  - c. Yes: HAc can react with both added acid and added base, so it works as a buffer
  - d. No: HAc is a strong acid, so it cannot be a buffer
  - Why: A weak acid alone is not a buffer. A tiny amount of Ac⁻ forms when HAc ionizes, far too little to draw, so added H⁺ has almost nothing to react with.
- `a-spectator` A buffer is made from CH₃COOH and CH₃COONa. Where do most of the CH₃COO⁻ ions come from?
  - **a. From the dissolved sodium acetate, with Na⁺ as a spectator**
  - b. From the acetic acid ionizing completely when it dissolves in the water
  - c. From water splitting into H⁺ and OH⁻
  - d. From Na⁺ combining with acetic acid
  - Why: Sodium acetate is a salt: it dissolves into Na⁺ and CH₃COO⁻. Acetic acid is weak and ionizes very little, so the salt supplies the base member.

### Checkpoint 4: The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

4 questions are drawn from a pool of 8.

- ★ `d-only` When can pH = pKa + log([base member] / [acid member]) be used?
  - **a. Only for a buffer with appreciable amounts of both members**
  - b. For any solution of a weak acid, even one with no conjugate base
  - c. For any acid solution, strong or weak
  - d. Only for a solution at exactly pH 7
  - Why: The equation is built for a buffer. It needs appreciable amounts of BOTH members. For a weak acid alone the ratio is almost zero and the equation does not work.
- `d-equal` A buffer has equal amounts of CH₃COOH and CH₃COO⁻. Its pH is
  - **a. 4.74, the pKa of CH₃COOH**
  - b. 7.00
  - c. 2.88
  - d. 9.26
  - Why: Equal amounts make the ratio 1, and log(1) = 0, so pH = pKa = 4.74.
- `d-ten` A CH₃COOH / CH₃COO⁻ buffer has 10 times as much CH₃COO⁻ as CH₃COOH. Its pH is
  - **a. 5.74**
  - b. 3.74
  - c. 4.74
  - d. 47.4
  - Why: The ratio is 10 and log(10) = 1, so pH = 4.74 + 1 = 5.74. More base member means a higher pH.
- ★ `d-calc` A buffer has 0.30 mol CH₃COOH and 0.20 mol CH₃COO⁻ (pKa 4.74). What is its pH?
  - **a. 4.57**
  - b. 4.91
  - c. 4.74
  - d. 4.44
  - Why: pH = 4.74 + log(0.20 / 0.30) = 4.74 + log(0.67) = 4.74 − 0.18 = 4.57. The base member goes on top.
- `d-after` A buffer has 10 HAc and 10 Ac⁻ (pKa 4.74). Then 4 particles of H⁺ are added. Which pH does the equation give?
  - **a. 4.38**
  - b. 5.11
  - c. 4.74
  - d. 1.40
  - Why: The 4 H⁺ turn 4 Ac⁻ into HAc, so the new amounts are 14 HAc and 6 Ac⁻: pH = 4.74 + log(6 / 14) = 4.38. Reaction first, equation second.
- `d-no` For which solution can the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation NOT be used?
  - **a. 0.10 M CH₃COOH with nothing else**
  - b. 0.10 M CH₃COOH with 0.10 M CH₃COONa
  - c. 0.20 M NH₃ with 0.10 M NH₄Cl
  - d. 0.10 M CH₃COOH with 0.05 M CH₃COONa
  - Why: A solution of CH₃COOH alone has no appreciable CH₃COO⁻, so it is not a buffer. The other three have appreciable amounts of both members.
- `d-nh` A buffer has 0.20 M NH₃ and 0.10 M NH₄⁺. The pKa of NH₄⁺ is 9.26. What is its pH?
  - **a. 9.56**
  - b. 8.96
  - c. 9.26
  - d. 4.74
  - Why: The base member (NH₃) goes on top: pH = 9.26 + log(0.20 / 0.10) = 9.26 + 0.30 = 9.56.
- `d-dilute` A buffer is diluted so that both concentrations are cut in half. The equation predicts that the pH
  - **a. stays the same, because the ratio is the same**
  - b. rises by 0.30
  - c. falls by 0.30
  - d. goes to 7, because water has been added to the buffer
  - Why: The equation depends on the RATIO of base member to acid member. Halving both leaves the ratio, and the pH, unchanged. (A more dilute buffer can absorb less acid or base, though.)

### Checkpoint 5: Review

4 questions are drawn from a pool of 8.

- ★ `e-which` Which mixture is a buffer?
  - **a. 0.20 M NH₃ with 0.10 M NH₄Cl**
  - b. 0.20 M HCl with 0.10 M NaCl
  - c. 0.10 M NaOH with 0.10 M NaCl
  - d. 0.20 M CH₃COOH with 0.10 M NaCl
  - Why: NH₃ and NH₄⁺ are a weak base and its conjugate acid. The others are a strong acid, a strong base, or a weak acid with no conjugate base.
- `e-acid-nh` Strong acid is added to a buffer of NH₃ and NH₄⁺. Which reaction happens?
  - **a. H⁺ + NH₃ → NH₄⁺**
  - b. OH⁻ + NH₄⁺ → NH₃ + H₂O
  - c. H⁺ + NH₄⁺ → no reaction
  - d. H⁺ + Cl⁻ → HCl
  - Why: The weak base NH₃ is the base member, so it takes the added H⁺.
- `e-base-nh` Strong base is added to a buffer of NH₃ and NH₄⁺. Which reaction happens?
  - **a. OH⁻ + NH₄⁺ → NH₃ + H₂O**
  - b. H⁺ + NH₃ → NH₄⁺
  - c. OH⁻ + NH₃ → no reaction
  - d. OH⁻ + Cl⁻ → ClOH
  - Why: NH₄⁺ is the acid member, so it gives an H⁺ to the added OH⁻ and becomes NH₃.
- `e-limit` The diagram shows the CH₃COOH / CH₃COO⁻ buffer after 10 particles of HCl. What has happened to its ability to absorb more acid? *(diagram: 10 HAc, 10 Ac⁻, 10 HCl added)*
  - **a. It has run out: all 10 Ac⁻ are used up**
  - b. It is unchanged: a buffer can absorb unlimited acid
  - c. It has run out: all the HAc was used up
  - d. It is stronger now, because there is more HAc
  - Why: The base member absorbs the acid. With none left (0 Ac⁻), the next H⁺ stays free and the pH falls steeply. A buffer has a limited capacity.
- `e-curve` The graph shows pH against acid or base added to a buffer and to pure water. Which curve is the buffer? *(graph: buffer A and pure water B)*
  - **a. A: it stays nearly flat near the middle**
  - b. B: it stays nearly flat near the middle
  - c. A: it changes the most when acid is added
  - d. B: it changes the least when base is added
  - Why: The buffer (A) is nearly flat until a member runs out. Pure water (B) swings from about pH 1 to pH 13 with only a small amount of acid or base.
- `e-equal-nh` A buffer has equal amounts of NH₃ and NH₄⁺ (pKa of NH₄⁺ is 9.26). Its pH is
  - **a. 9.26**
  - b. 4.74
  - c. 7.00
  - d. 14.00
  - Why: Equal amounts: the ratio is 1, log(1) = 0, so pH = pKa = 9.26.
- ★ `e-no-hh` A student uses pH = pKa + log([A⁻] / [HA]) on a solution of only CH₃COOH. What goes wrong?
  - **a. There is almost no A⁻, so the ratio is close to zero**
  - b. Nothing goes wrong: the equation works for any weak acid in any solution
  - c. The equation only works for strong acids
  - d. CH₃COOH does not have a pKa
  - Why: The equation is only for a buffer, which has appreciable amounts of both members. For a weak acid alone, log of nearly zero is meaningless. You would need an equilibrium (ICE) calculation instead.
- `e-down` Which change makes the pH of a CH₃COOH / CH₃COO⁻ buffer go DOWN?
  - **a. The ratio of CH₃COO⁻ to CH₃COOH gets smaller, as with added acid**
  - b. The ratio of CH₃COO⁻ to CH₃COOH gets larger, as when strong base is added
  - c. The total amount of the buffer is doubled with the ratio unchanged
  - d. Some of the Na⁺ spectator is removed
  - Why: pH = pKa + log(base / acid). A smaller ratio gives a smaller log, so a lower pH. Adding strong acid turns Ac⁻ into HAc and lowers the ratio.
