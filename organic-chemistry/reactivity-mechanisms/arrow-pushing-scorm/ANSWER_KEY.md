**Say it in words.** Bromide is the **nucleophile**; the carbocation is the **electrophile**. The O of methanol is the **nucleophile**. The pi bond’s pair moves onto O, so the carbon never has a **fifth bond**. The C–Br bond breaks **heterolytically**: Br⁻ leaves with both electrons as the **leaving group**. The O⁻ pushes a pair down to re-form the C=O, which pushes out Cl⁻, the **leaving group**. One pattern: loss of a leaving group, drawn with **two** arrows. Acetone acts as a **base** and takes a **proton** from H₃O⁺. Water acts as a **base**. The O–H pair stays behind on O: a proton moves **without** its electrons. The **pi bond** is the base. The new carbocation is **tertiary**, the more stable one. A **hydride shift**: the H moves with its pair, and a 2° carbocation becomes a more stable **3°** one. A **methyl shift**: the CH₃ moves with its pair to the C⁺. That is a carbocation **rearrangement**. Two patterns at the same time, in one step: a **concerted** step, with one **transition state** and no intermediate.

## Checkpoints

### Checkpoint 1: energy and bonds

4 per attempt. Always: a-break, a-favor.

- **a-break** Breaking a bond is: *Endothermic: energy goes into the system* Bond breaking is endothermic. The bonding electrons sit low in energy, so the surroundings must give the system energy to pull the atoms apart.
- **a-favor** A reaction with ΔG < 0 is: *Exergonic and thermodynamically favorable* ΔG < 0 means the products are lower in free energy than the reactants: exergonic, and thermodynamically favorable (K > 1).
- **a-homo** Homolytic cleavage of a bond gives: *Two radicals, one electron each* Homolytic: the bond splits evenly, one electron to each atom (two fish-hook arrows). Each atom has an unpaired electron: two radicals.
- **a-hetero** Heterolytic cleavage is drawn with: *One curved arrow: both electrons to one atom* Heterolytic: both electrons of the bond go to one atom, shown by one curved arrow with a full head. It makes a cation and an anion: ions.
- **a-647** For a reaction with ΔH° < 0, which must be true? (Klein 6.47) *The bonds broken are weaker than the bonds formed* Breaking the old bonds takes energy in; making the new ones gives energy out. If more comes out than goes in (exothermic), the bonds formed must be stronger than the bonds broken.
- **a-endo** What does this energy diagram show? *Endergonic: not thermodynamically favorable* The products sit higher than the reactants, so ΔG > 0: endergonic, not thermodynamically favorable, and K < 1.
- **a-valley** On an energy diagram, a valley between two peaks is: *An intermediate* Every peak is a transition state and every valley between peaks is an intermediate (Klein Figure 6.21).
- **a-ts** A transition state: *Has partial bonds and cannot be isolated* A transition state is the top of a hill: bonds are partly broken and partly formed at once. The reaction passes through it; it cannot be isolated. An intermediate sits in a valley and lives for a short time.
- **a-dS** Which change makes ΔS of the system positive? *One molecule breaks into two* More molecules, or an open chain instead of a ring, means more ways to arrange the system: higher entropy (Klein Figure 6.7).
- **a-highT** ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0. When is the reaction thermodynamically favorable? *Only at high temperature* ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. With ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0, the −TΔS term wins only when T is large enough: favorable at high temperature.
- **a-fast** Which one tells you how fast a reaction goes? *Eₐ, the height of the hill* Kinetics (how fast) depends on the activation energy, the climb from the reactants to the transition state. Thermodynamics (ΔG) only says which side is favored at equilibrium.
- **a-make** When a bond forms, energy is: *Released: bond making is exothermic* Bond making is exothermic: the electrons drop into a low-energy bonding orbital, and the system gives that energy to the surroundings.
- **a-K** A reaction that is thermodynamically favorable has: *K > 1* ΔG < 0 goes with K > 1: at equilibrium there are more products than reactants.

### Checkpoint 2: nucleophiles and electrophiles

4 per attempt. Always: b-nuc.

- **b-nuc** A nucleophile is: *Electron-rich: it gives a pair of electrons* A nucleophile ("nucleus lover") is electron-rich and donates a pair of electrons: a Lewis base. An electrophile ("electron lover") is electron-poor and accepts one: a Lewis acid.
- **b-lewis** An electrophile is also: *A Lewis acid* An electrophile accepts a pair of electrons, which is what a Lewis acid does.
- **b-k8a** Will this compound act as a nucleophile or an electrophile? *Electrophile* Br pulls electron density from the carbon it is bonded to (induction), so that carbon is δ+: an alkyl halide is an electrophile.
- **b-k8f** Will this ion act as a nucleophile or an electrophile? *Nucleophile* An alkoxide has a negative charge on an O with lone pairs: a strong nucleophile.
- **b-k8d** Will cyclohexene act as a nucleophile or an electrophile? *Nucleophile* The pi bond of an alkene is a region of high electron density, so alkenes are nucleophiles.
- **b-k8c** Will cyclopentanone act as a nucleophile or an electrophile? *Electrophile* The carbon of a C=O is δ+ by induction and by resonance, so a carbonyl group is an electrophile.
- **b-empty** Why is the carbon of a carbocation electrophilic? *It has an empty p orbital* A carbocation has only three bonds and no lone pair. Its empty p orbital can accept a pair of electrons.
- **b-polar** Why are I⁻ and HS⁻ such strong nucleophiles? *They are large and polarizable* Polarizability: the electron cloud of a large atom (I, S) distorts easily toward an electrophile. Klein: I⁻ and HS⁻ are among the strongest nucleophiles known.
- **b-weak** Water and alcohols (ROH) are: *Weak nucleophiles* Their O has lone pairs, so they can attack, but with no negative charge they are weak nucleophiles.
- **b-spect** In a mechanism, NaOH, LiOH and KOH are all: *The same nucleophile: hydroxide* Na⁺, Li⁺ and K⁺ are counter-ions that just balance the charge. All three supply the same nucleophile, HO⁻.
- **b-count** How many electrophilic centres does this compound have? *2* Two: the C of the C=O and the C bonded to Cl. The OH is its one nucleophilic centre (Klein SkillBuilder 6.2).
- **b-co** The carbon of a C=O is electrophilic because: *O pulls electron density away from it* O is more electronegative than C (induction), and a resonance structure puts + on the carbon. Either way the carbon is δ+.

- **c-r3** Will this carbocation rearrange? *No* It is 2°. A hydride shift from either neighbour gives another 2° (or a 1°) carbocation, which is no more stable. So it does not rearrange.
- **c-hmove** In a hydride shift, what moves? *An H, with its pair of electrons* A hydride shift moves H⁻: the H and both electrons of its bond. A proton (H⁺) moves without its electrons, in a proton transfer.
- **c-why** Why does a carbocation rearrange? *To become a more stable cation* Only if the shift makes a more stable carbocation (for example 2° to 3°). The new one is still a carbocation.
